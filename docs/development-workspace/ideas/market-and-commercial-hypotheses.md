# Market and commercial hypotheses

This note records early distribution, positioning, and pricing hypotheses. None
of the markets, names, plans, quotas, or packaging below is an approved product
or commercial commitment.

## Distribution hypothesis

Research may remain the strongest long-term product context, but early adoption
also depends on who is practical to reach. Existing relationships with business
owners could provide faster access to the first useful product trials than a
theoretically ideal market with no natural distribution path.

Those early users do not need to define the eventual customer base. They can
still reveal whether the underlying interaction model works, which terms cause
confusion, and where real processes diverge from the intended common path.

The corresponding hypothesis is:

- keep one core product rather than creating research and business forks;
- test it through the networks that are presently reachable;
- offer market-specific examples and entry points in marketing; and
- prevent industry-specific requests from pulling the product beyond the core
  interaction boundary in
  [[study-interaction-product-direction|Study interaction product direction]].

Requests for invoicing, inventory, a general CRM, or other unrelated operations
should not enter the core roadmap merely because an early user asks for them.

## Positioning and language

A broad promise such as “software for everyone” would be unfocused. A more
coherent cross-market promise is collecting structured responses from people
and automating the bounded follow-up around those interactions.

Potential entry points include research studies, customer research, client
onboarding, and lead qualification. These examples should demonstrate the same
product capability rather than imply separate vertical products.

Interface language needs testing rather than an immediate rename. “Study” and
“participant” provide valuable research context, while “project,” “workflow,”
“respondent,” or “contact” may travel more naturally across markets. The current
domain also distinguishes respondents, participants, identities, and subjects
for concrete access and continuity reasons; a marketing term should not erase
those implementation distinctions accidentally.

## Commercial model hypothesis

An early commercial model needs to support two different goals:

1. make adoption easy enough to learn and build trust; and
2. earn enough from higher-value or higher-usage customers to sustain the
   service.

Core surveys, screening, consent interactions, longitudinal follow-up, and
lightweight automation are candidate product differentiators. The hypothesis is
to avoid disabling that identity at entry level and instead charge for scale,
convenience, collaboration, governance, or support.

| Candidate plan | Possible audience | Possible packaging boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free | Student or small first project | One active study and moderate usage |
| Individual Pro | PhD student, independent researcher, or power user | More active studies, participants, storage, email, analytics, and support |
| Team | Research lab or operating team | Shared workspace, collaboration, and roles |
| Enterprise | University or institution | SSO, governance, administration, and institutional support |

This table describes a progression to test, not decided tiers. Active
participants, message volume, storage, and compute may align price with service
cost, but any usage metric must also be predictable and easy for a customer to
understand. Exact prices and limits should follow evidence rather than being
recorded here as guesses.

The free plan would primarily be an acquisition path. An individual paid plan
could provide an earlier sustainability path before team collaboration or
institutional governance is complete.

## Evidence needed

- Interviews with reachable researchers and business users using the same core
  prototype.
- Observation of whether research-specific language helps or obstructs each
  group.
- Evidence that the common interaction workflow solves a recurring problem.
- Willingness-to-pay evidence from individuals, labs, and institutions.
- Measured storage, email, compute, and support costs at realistic usage levels.
- Tests of whether participant-based limits feel aligned, predictable, and
  ethically appropriate.

## Decisions not made

- The primary initial market and eventual market focus.
- Whether any top-level product nouns should change.
- Which use cases appear on the main marketing surface.
- Plan names, prices, limits, feature entitlements, or overage rules.
- Whether the product should use usage-based pricing at all.
